CHAPTER 01

Supervised Learning

"Most of what you'll learn in this module," the friend says, "is a flavor of supervised learning." Show the system enough examples with correct answers attached, and it learns the mapping from input to output well enough to guess correctly on new inputs.

In supervised learning, every training example is a pair: features (the input data) and a label (the correct answer). The model's whole job during training is to get better and better at predicting the label from the features.

A Labeled Training Example
Features
house size: 1800 sqft
bedrooms: 3
location score: 7.2
→
Label
sale price: $312,000
  • Every supervised example pairs input features with the correct output — this pairing is what "supervises" the learning.
  • Two main tasks fall under supervised learning, covered next: regression (predicting a number) and classification (predicting a category).
  • Every algorithm from Chapter 6 onward — decision trees, random forests, SVMs, neural networks — can be used in a supervised way.
Takeaway: If your data comes with "correct answers" already attached, you're in supervised learning territory — the rest of this module is mostly variations on this one theme.

CHAPTER 02

Unsupervised Learning

"Now take away the labels entirely," the friend says. "No correct answers, no supervision — just a pile of data, and the question: does this data have any natural structure at all?"

The scatter of dots below has no labels. Click the button and watch a real k-means clustering algorithm — the same kind used in customer segmentation — discover the groups on its own, live.

Live K-Means Clustering
Ungrouped data — click Run Clustering to find structure.
  • Clustering groups similar data points together based purely on their features — no labels required.
  • The algorithm you just ran assigns each point to its nearest cluster center, then moves each center to the average of its assigned points, repeating until stable.
  • Real use case: grouping customers into natural shopping segments without anyone manually labeling a single customer.
Takeaway: Unsupervised learning answers a different question than supervised learning — not "what's the right answer," but "is there a pattern here at all?"

CHAPTER 03

Reinforcement Learning

"Neither of the first two fits a game-playing AI," the friend points out. "There's no fixed 'correct move' label, and it's not just finding clusters. It's learning by doing — and getting better because doing badly hurts."

An agent takes actions in an environment, receives a reward (or penalty) for each action, and gradually learns which actions lead to better long-term outcomes.

The Agent-Environment Loop
Average reward per episode, over training \u2014 illustrative
  • No labels, no fixed dataset — the agent generates its own experience by acting and observing what happens.
  • Reward shapes behavior gradually: actions that lead to higher reward get reinforced over many repeated attempts.
  • Real use cases: game-playing agents, robotics control, and resource management systems that improve through repeated trials.
Takeaway: Reinforcement learning trades labeled examples for lived experience — the "right answer" is discovered through consequences, not handed over in advance.

CHAPTER 04

Regression

"First real supervised technique," the friend says, sliding over a scatter of dots. "Your job: draw the line that best fits these points." Alex reaches for the sliders.

Regression predicts a continuous number, like a price or a temperature. The simplest version, linear regression, just fits the straight line that minimizes the total prediction error across all the data points. Try it yourself, then let the computer do it properly.

Fit a Line to the Data
1.0 3
Total squared error: \u2014
  • Smaller total error means a better fit — the "Fit Best Line" button uses real least-squares math to find the minimum, not a guess.
  • Regression predicts a number, not a category — house prices, temperatures, stock values, exam scores.
  • Many algorithms later in this course (including some quantum ones in Module 9) can be used for regression, not just classification.
Takeaway: "Best fit" has a precise mathematical meaning — the line that minimizes total squared error — not just a line that looks about right.

CHAPTER 05

Classification

"Same setup," the friend says, "different question. Instead of predicting a number, predict which of two groups a point belongs to." Two colors of dots appear, tangled together in the middle.

Classification predicts a category instead of a number — spam or not spam, cat or dog, malicious or benign. Drag the slider to move the decision boundary and watch accuracy change live.

Find the Best Decision Boundary
50
Accuracy: \u2014
  • The decision boundary is the line a classifier uses to separate predicted classes — everything on one side gets one label, the other side gets the other.
  • Accuracy measures the percentage of points correctly classified by a given boundary position.
  • Real classifiers (Decision Trees, SVMs, Neural Networks — all coming up) learn this boundary automatically from training data, instead of a human dragging a slider.
Takeaway: Every classification algorithm in this module is, underneath, searching for a good decision boundary — they just search for it in very different ways.

CHAPTER 06

Decision Trees

"Here's an algorithm you already use," the friend says, "every time you decide whether to bring an umbrella." A decision tree is just a flowchart of yes/no questions, learned automatically from data instead of written by hand.

Click through the questions below and watch the path light up to a final decision.

Walk Through a Decision Tree
Answer the questions above to reach a decision.
  • Each internal node asks a yes/no question about a feature; each leaf gives a final prediction.
  • Trees are trained by finding the questions that best split the training data into purer, more homogeneous groups at each step.
  • Big advantage: a trained tree's logic is easy for a human to read and explain, unlike many other algorithms in this module.
Takeaway: A decision tree is basically a learned flowchart — readable by humans, and the building block for the more powerful technique in the next chapter.

CHAPTER 07

Random Forest

"One tree can be wrong," the friend says. "Ask a hundred slightly different trees the same question and take a vote, and the mistakes tend to cancel out." That's the entire idea behind a random forest.

A random forest trains many decision trees, each on a slightly different random slice of the data, then combines their predictions — typically by majority vote for classification.

Five Trees Vote
  • Diversity is the point — each tree sees a different random slice of data, so their mistakes tend to be different too.
  • Majority vote (classification) or averaging (regression) combines all the trees' outputs into one final, typically more reliable prediction.
  • Random forests are usually more accurate and more resistant to overfitting than any single decision tree alone.
Takeaway: "Ask a crowd instead of one expert" is a surprisingly powerful and general idea — it shows up again later in this course as an ensemble technique.

CHAPTER 08

Support Vector Machine

"Classification chapter's slider found a good boundary," the friend says. "An SVM finds the boundary with maximum breathing room on both sides — the widest possible margin."

A Support Vector Machine (SVM) doesn't just separate the classes — it maximizes the margin, the distance between the boundary and the closest point from each class. Those closest points are called support vectors, and they alone determine where the boundary sits.

Maximum Margin Boundary
Highlighted points = support vectors \u2014 they alone define the boundary's position.
  • A wider margin tends to generalize better to new, unseen data than a boundary that barely squeezes between the classes.
  • Only the support vectors matter for the final boundary — every other point could move slightly without changing it at all.
  • SVMs can also handle non-linearly-separable data using a technique called the "kernel trick" — the same underlying idea behind Module 9's quantum kernel methods.
Takeaway: Margin-maximizing isn't just a nice extra — it's a principled reason to prefer one valid boundary over another, and it will resurface almost word-for-word in Module 9's quantum kernel chapter.

CHAPTER 09

Neural Networks

"Loosely inspired by the brain," the friend says, "but don't take that too literally — it's really just layers of simple math functions, connected by adjustable numbers called weights." Click below and watch a signal move through one.

A neural network is built from layers of neurons: an input layer (the raw features), one or more hidden layers (where the real computation happens), and an output layer (the final prediction). Every connection carries a weight, adjusted during training.

A Small Neural Network
  • Weights are what gets learned — training adjusts every connection's weight to reduce prediction error, usually via an algorithm called backpropagation.
  • Hidden layers let the network build up increasingly abstract combinations of the input features.
  • Neural networks are extremely flexible and now the backbone of most modern AI — including the quantum neural networks in Module 9.
Takeaway: A neural network is a general-purpose function approximator built from simple, repeated pieces — the complexity comes from how many pieces you stack, which is exactly next chapter's topic.

CHAPTER 10

Deep Learning

Closing chapter. "One more slider," the friend says, "and it's the one that explains the last decade of AI progress." Drag it, and watch the network from last chapter grow noticeably deeper.

Deep learning simply means a neural network with many hidden layers stacked between input and output. More layers can capture more abstract, complex patterns — at the cost of needing more data and more computing power to train well.

Shallow vs. Deep
1
  • "Deep" just means more hidden layers — not a different kind of algorithm, but a scaled-up version of last chapter's idea.
  • 2012 onward is when deep networks became practical, once enough labeled data and GPU computing power arrived at the same time.
  • The tradeoff: deeper networks are often more powerful, but need more data, more compute, and more care to train without overfitting.
Takeaway: This closes the loop on Module 1's history chapter — 2012's breakthrough was, mechanically, exactly this slider being pushed further than anyone had successfully managed before.
