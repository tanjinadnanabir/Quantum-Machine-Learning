CHAPTER 01

Limitations of Classical Computing

"Before I show you what's different," the friend says, "let's see exactly where classical computers hit a wall — because quantum computing only matters where this wall is real."

Every classical bit is either 0 or 1 — nothing in between. Adding one more bit doesn't just add one more possibility, it doubles the total number of combinations the computer might need to check. That doubling, repeated enough times, outruns any amount of raw speed.

State Space Explosion
8
—
—
  • Classical computers check one definite combination at a time — a bit string is 0110...1, full stop, never a blend of possibilities.
  • 2ⁿ growth is brutal: 50 bits alone already produce more combinations than a supercomputer could enumerate in the age of the universe.
  • Problems like simulating a moderately sized molecule or factoring a huge number have exactly this kind of exponential possibility space — which is precisely where quantum computers start to matter.
Takeaway: It isn't that classical computers are slow. It's that some problems' possibility space grows faster than any amount of added speed can ever outrun.

CHAPTER 02

Quantum Computing Fundamentals

"So a different wall needs a different kind of tool," the friend says. "Not a faster bit — a fundamentally different building block, borrowed from quantum mechanics itself."

A quantum computer uses the rules of quantum mechanics — superposition, entanglement, and interference — to represent and process information in a way no classical machine can imitate. It is not a faster general-purpose computer; it's a specialized tool that only pulls ahead on certain kinds of problems.

Two Computing Paradigms
  • Three ingredients power this whole module: superposition (Ch. 4), entanglement (Ch. 5), and the speedups they enable (Ch. 6) — each gets its own chapter, one at a time.
  • Quantum computers don't replace classical computers — most everyday computing tasks get no benefit at all, and classical machines remain better for them.
  • The basic unit of quantum information is the qubit — meet it properly in the very next chapter.
Takeaway: Quantum computing is a specialized toolset, not a universal upgrade — the rest of this module builds up exactly which tools it adds, and why they matter.

CHAPTER 03

Qubits

"Meet the basic unit itself," the friend says, sketching a sphere in the air. "A classical bit is a light switch — on or off. A qubit is this whole sphere — every point on its surface is a legal state."

A qubit is a two-level quantum system whose state is written α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. The numbers α and β are amplitudes; squaring their size gives the probability of measuring 0 or 1. Drag the angle below and watch the state move around the Bloch sphere — the standard picture of a single qubit's state.

The Bloch Sphere (2D Slice)
0°
—
  • θ = 0° is the pure state |0⟩ (north pole); θ = 180° is the pure state |1⟩ (south pole) — everything in between is a genuine superposition.
  • Squaring the amplitude — not the amplitude itself — gives the measurement probability, which is why the readout above uses cos²(θ/2) and sin²(θ/2).
  • Physically, a qubit can be built from a supercooled superconducting circuit, a trapped ion, a single photon, and more — Chapter 7 compares the leading approaches.
Takeaway: A qubit's state is a point on a sphere, not a switch — "somewhere between 0 and 1" is exactly as real a state as 0 or 1 themselves, right up until it's measured.

CHAPTER 04

Superposition

"Now push that dial and actually watch superposition do something," the friend says. "Before you measure, both outcomes are simultaneously true — not secretly decided and just hidden from you. That's the part people usually get wrong."

Set the split between |0⟩ and |1⟩ below, then hit measure. Every measurement forces the qubit to give one definite classical answer, chosen at random, exactly according to the probabilities you set — this is how a real qubit is read out.

Set a Superposition, Then Measure
50%
—
No measurements yet.
  • Before measurement, the qubit genuinely holds both possibilities at once — a real, testable physical fact, not just uncertainty on your part.
  • Measurement collapses the superposition to one classical outcome, at random, weighted by the probabilities you set with the slider.
  • Run it many times and the tally converges toward those exact probabilities — quantum algorithms are built around extracting useful answers from this kind of repeated sampling.
Takeaway: Superposition isn't "the computer secretly already knows the answer" — it's a real mixture of possibilities that only resolves the instant you measure.

CHAPTER 05

Entanglement

"One qubit was interesting," the friend says. "Two entangled qubits are where it gets genuinely strange." Two qubits are prepared together so their fates are linked — measure one, and you instantly know the other, no matter how far apart they are.

Click below to create an entangled pair and measure both. Each individual outcome is still perfectly random — but compare qubit A and qubit B across many trials, and a pattern that no classical coin-flipping could produce shows up immediately.

Create a Bell Pair & Measure
Run a trial to entangle and measure a pair of qubits.
  • Entangled qubits share one joint description — you cannot fully describe qubit A's state without also describing qubit B's, even before either is measured.
  • Individually, each outcome is still random — the correlation is only visible when you compare the two results side by side, exactly like the trial log above.
  • No faster-than-light messaging: neither side can choose which outcome they get, so entanglement can't be used to send information — but it is a real physical resource used in quantum algorithms and quantum cryptography.
Takeaway: Entanglement links outcomes, not information — the correlation is real and instant, but neither observer can control it, so no signal can ride along with it.

CHAPTER 06

Quantum Advantage

"This is the payoff," the friend says. "Superposition and entanglement together let certain algorithms need dramatically fewer steps — not a small speed bump, a fundamentally different growth rate."

Grover's algorithm finds a marked item in an unsorted list of N items in about √N steps, versus roughly N/2 steps for the best classical approach — a proven quadratic speedup. Shor's algorithm goes further, factoring huge numbers exponentially faster than any known classical method, which is why it matters so much for cryptography.

Classical Search vs. Grover's Algorithm
10,000
—
  • √N vs. N/2 — as the slider above shows, the gap between classical and Grover's search widens dramatically as the list grows.
  • Shor's algorithm factors large numbers exponentially faster than classical methods, underlying much of the discussion around quantum computing and encryption.
  • Quantum advantage is task-specific: for everyday computing — spreadsheets, browsing, word processing — a classical computer is just as good, or better.
Takeaway: A quantum computer doesn't run everything faster — it runs a specific, well-defined class of problems fundamentally differently, and search and factoring are the two clearest proven examples.

CHAPTER 07

Real Quantum Hardware

"None of this stays theoretical," the friend says. "Real machines exist right now, built from a handful of competing physical approaches — click through them." Numbers below are approximate and evolving quickly; the tradeoffs are the durable part.

Today's machines are often called NISQ devices — Noisy, Intermediate-Scale Quantum. They're powerful research tools, but still limited by noise and decoherence, well short of the scale needed to run Shor's algorithm on cryptographically relevant numbers. Click each card to flip it.

Physical Qubit Approaches — Click to Flip
  • No single approach has "won" yet — superconducting, trapped-ion, photonic, and neutral-atom systems each trade off coherence time, gate speed, and scalability differently.
  • Raw qubit count is a headline number — quality measures like error rate and quantum volume matter just as much for what a machine can actually compute.
  • Every real device built on these approaches is running exactly the physics from Chapters 3 through 5: qubits, superposition, and entanglement, just with real-world noise added in.
Takeaway: The theory from Chapters 2 through 6 is running on real, imperfect, rapidly improving machines today — Module 5 opens the hood on exactly how you program one.
