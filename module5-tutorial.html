CHAPTER 02

Complex Numbers

"One more piece of notation," the friend says. "Quantum amplitudes aren't just any numbers — they're complex numbers, with a real part and an imaginary part."

A complex number is written a + bi, where i = √-1. Plotted on the plane below, a is the horizontal position and b is the vertical position. Its magnitude (distance from the origin) is what determines measurement probability — its phase (angle) is invisible to a single measurement but crucial once qubits interact.

The Complex Plane (Argand Diagram)
0.7
0.7
—
  • Magnitude |a + bi| = √(a² + b²) — squaring the magnitude of a quantum amplitude gives its measurement probability, the same Born rule from Module 4.
  • Phase is the angle the arrow makes with the horizontal axis — two states with the same magnitude but different phase are physically distinct, even though a single measurement can't tell them apart.
  • Every ket you'll write from Chapter 3 onward can have complex-number amplitudes — this module keeps most worked examples real-valued for clarity, but the machinery generalizes directly.
Takeaway: Magnitude tells you "how likely"; phase tells you "in what way" — and quantum interference, later in this course, is entirely a story about phase.

CHAPTER 03

Dirac Notation

"Physicists got tired of writing full column vectors everywhere," the friend says, "so they invented shorthand." A ket |ψ⟩ is a state vector. A bra ⟨φ| is its mirror image, used for computing overlaps.

The overlap between two states, written ⟨φ|ψ⟩, is just their vectors' dot product. Its squared size tells you how "alike" two states are — 1 means identical, 0 means completely distinguishable. Pick two kets below and compute their overlap.

Compute an Inner Product ⟨φ|ψ⟩
Pick a bra and a ket above.
  • |0⟩ = [1, 0] and |1⟩ = [0, 1] are the two basic building-block kets — every other single-qubit state is some combination of these two.
  • |+⟩ = [1/√2, 1/√2] and |−⟩ = [1/√2, −1/√2] are equal superpositions — you'll build both of these yourself with a gate in Chapter 4.
  • ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ always equals 1 for a valid quantum state — this is exactly the "probabilities add up to 100%" rule from Module 4, written in matrix form.
Takeaway: Bra-ket notation isn't new physics — it's just a compact way to write vectors and dot products, and ⟨φ|ψ⟩ is the single most useful calculation in this whole module.

CHAPTER 04

Quantum Gates

"Time to actually transform a qubit," the friend says. "A gate is just a matrix — but a special kind: it has to preserve total probability, which means it has to be unitary."

Start from |0⟩ and apply a gate below. Watch the resulting amplitudes — and the resulting probabilities — update from real matrix multiplication, exactly like Chapter 1's vector demo.

Apply a Gate to |0⟩
|\u03C8\u27E9 = 1.00|0\u27E9 + 0.00|1\u27E9
P(|0\u27E9) = 100.0% P(|1\u27E9) = 0.0%
  • X swaps the amplitudes of |0⟩ and |1⟩ — the quantum version of a classical NOT gate.
  • Z leaves probabilities unchanged but flips the sign (phase) of the |1⟩ amplitude — invisible to a single measurement, but not to later gates.
  • H (Hadamard) turns |0⟩ into |+⟩, an equal 50/50 superposition — this single gate is how nearly every quantum algorithm gets started.
Takeaway: Every gate you just clicked is unitary — the total probability (P(|0⟩) + P(|1⟩)) stays exactly 100% no matter which one you apply, which is exactly what "valid quantum gate" means.

CHAPTER 05

Quantum Circuits

"One gate is a sentence," the friend says. "A circuit is a whole paragraph — gates applied one after another, left to right, each one acting on whatever state the last one left behind."

Build a tiny circuit below by clicking gates onto the strip in order. The state updates after each one — exactly the same matrix multiplication as Chapter 4, just chained together.

Build a Circuit, Starting from |0⟩
|\u03C8\u27E9 = 1.00|0\u27E9 + 0.00|1\u27E9
P(|0\u27E9) = 100.0% P(|1\u27E9) = 0.0%
  • Order matters: try H then Z versus Z then H — same two gates, different final state, because matrix multiplication isn't commutative.
  • Try H, then X, then H — this three-gate circuit does something a single gate can't achieve alone (worth predicting before you click).
  • Real quantum programs are exactly this: sequences of a handful of basic gates, chosen to steer a state toward a useful final answer.
Takeaway: A circuit diagram is a recipe read left to right — and because gate order changes the outcome, circuit design is really about finding the right sequence, not just the right ingredients.

CHAPTER 06

Bloch Sphere

"Module 4 showed you a slice of this sphere," the friend says. "Now you have the matrices to earn the full picture — two angles, not one, because a qubit's state needs both magnitude and phase to pin down."

The angle θ (theta) controls how much |0⟩ versus |1⟩ — exactly like Module 4. The angle φ (phi) adds the phase information from Chapter 2, rotating the state around the vertical axis without changing its measurement probabilities at all.

Full Bloch Sphere — θ and φ
90°
0°
—
  • θ alone determines measurement probability — P(|0⟩) = cos²(θ/2), P(|1⟩) = sin²(θ/2), unaffected by φ at all.
  • φ moves the point around the equator's "latitude circle" at a fixed θ — this is exactly the invisible-to-single-measurement phase from Chapter 2.
  • The four preset buttons are the four states you've been computing with all module — |0⟩ and |1⟩ at the poles, |+⟩ and |−⟩ on the equator, 180° apart in φ.
Takeaway: Every gate from Chapter 4 is secretly a specific rotation of this sphere — X flips pole to pole, Z spins around the vertical axis, H swings a pole out to the equator.
