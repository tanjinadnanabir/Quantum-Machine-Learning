CHAPTER 01

What is AI?

Alex asks a friend who works in tech: "So what actually is AI?" The friend laughs. "Honestly? The definition keeps moving." Whatever computers can't quite do yet gets called AI; the moment it becomes routine, people stop calling it that.

Artificial Intelligence is the field of building systems that perform tasks which normally require human intelligence — recognizing images, understanding language, making decisions, learning from experience. It is a moving target on purpose: yesterday's cutting-edge AI (like a chess engine) is today's unremarkable software feature.

Try the game below — for each example, guess whether it counts as "AI" before checking the answer.

Spot the AI
  • AI is not one thing. It is an umbrella term covering many different techniques aimed at the same broad goal: making machines act intelligently.
  • "Intelligent" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Most AI systems are narrowly skilled at one task, not broadly smart the way a person is.
  • The "AI effect": once a capability becomes common and well-understood, people often stop calling it AI at all — a moving goalpost worth noticing.
Takeaway: Before worrying about whether AI is good or bad, get comfortable with the fact that the word itself is a fuzzy, shifting category — not a single technology.

CHAPTER 02

History and Evolution of AI

Alex assumed AI was a recent invention — maybe ten years old. The friend pulls up a timeline going back over seventy years, full of excitement, disappointment, and quiet comebacks. "AI has boomed and gone bust more than once already," the friend says. "This isn't its first rodeo."

The field has moved in waves — bursts of excitement (and funding) followed by "AI winters" when progress stalled and interest cooled, followed eventually by a fresh breakthrough that restarted everything.

Seven Decades, at a Glance
1950
Turing asks "Can machines think?"
1956
Dartmouth Conference coins "Artificial Intelligence"
1970s-80s
First "AI winters" as early hype outran results
1997
Deep Blue beats world chess champion Kasparov
2012
Deep learning breaks through on image recognition
2020s
Large language models reach the mainstream
  • 1956 is usually treated as AI's official "birth" — the Dartmouth Conference, where the term itself was coined.
  • AI winters happened when early promises (like fully automated translation) outran what the technology of the time could deliver.
  • 2012 onward marked a real, lasting shift — deep learning finally delivered on decades-old ambitions, thanks to more data and more computing power arriving at once.
Takeaway: AI's history is not a straight line of progress — it is a story of overpromising, disappointment, and eventually, real breakthroughs arriving later than expected but bigger than hoped.

CHAPTER 03

Types of AI

"Every AI you've ever used," the friend says, "is what's called Narrow AI." Alex looks confused — narrow doesn't sound like the word people use for AI that seems to write essays and hold conversations. "That's exactly the confusion this chapter is going to clear up."

The most useful split isn't about how impressive a system seems — it's about how broadly its intelligence actually generalizes.

Three Categories, Only One of Which Exists Today
Exists Today
Narrow AI (ANI)

Skilled at one specific task — chess, translation, image recognition, even writing text — but with no general understanding outside that task. Every AI system in use today, including large language models, falls in this category.

Hypothetical
General AI (AGI)

Would match human-level intelligence across essentially any task, the way a person can learn to cook, do math, and hold a conversation without being separately "trained" for each. This does not exist yet, despite how some marketing language talks about current systems.

Speculative
Superintelligence (ASI)

A hypothetical intelligence exceeding human ability across every domain. Purely speculative and often the subject of debate among researchers about if or when it might be possible.

  • Narrow AI can look extremely impressive within its lane and still have zero understanding outside it.
  • General AI is the sci-fi idea of a single system that can do what any human can do — genuinely useful as a concept, not something in production today.
  • Confusing narrow AI's fluency (like a chatbot's conversational skill) for general intelligence is one of the most common misunderstandings about the field.
Takeaway: When someone says "AI," ask yourself which type they actually mean — the gap between narrow and general AI is the single most important distinction in this whole field.

CHAPTER 04

AI Applications

"Once you know to look for it," the friend says, "narrow AI is everywhere — not just in flashy demos." Alex starts noticing it in the next five minutes: the phone's keyboard predicting words, a streaming app's recommendations, a spam folder quietly doing its job.

Narrow AI has quietly become infrastructure across nearly every industry. Tap each card below.

Where AI Already Shows Up — Tap Each Card
  • Most AI applications are invisible by design — spam filters and fraud detection work best when you never notice them at all.
  • The same underlying techniques repeat across industries — pattern recognition and prediction show up in healthcare, finance, and entertainment alike.
  • This course is specifically about the machine learning techniques (Module 2 onward) that power almost every example above.
Takeaway: AI is less a single dramatic invention and more a toolkit that has quietly spread into nearly every industry, one narrow task at a time.

CHAPTER 05

Future of AI

Last question before Alex moves on: "So where is this all going?" The friend pauses. "I'll tell you what I think — and exactly how sure I am about each part," the same honesty this whole course tries to model, right from Module 1.

Every item below is labeled by confidence, not certainty.

Three Horizons, Honestly Labeled
Near-Term
  • PlausibleAI tools becoming a normal part of everyday work, the way spreadsheets did
  • PlausibleContinued rapid improvement in language and image generation
Mid-Term
  • SpeculativeAI systems handling increasingly complex, multi-step tasks reliably
  • SpeculativeClearer regulation and standards as adoption grows
Long-Term
  • SpeculativeProgress toward more general-purpose AI capabilities
  • SpeculativeContinued, genuine scientific disagreement about timelines
  • Nobody knows for certain — reputable researchers disagree meaningfully about how fast capabilities will improve.
  • Confident predictions in either direction (utopia or catastrophe) deserve extra scrutiny — this field has a long history of both being wrong.
Takeaway: The most trustworthy answer to "what happens next with AI" is usually the one that admits genuine uncertainty — a habit worth carrying through the rest of this course.
